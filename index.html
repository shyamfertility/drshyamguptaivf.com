Fertility Medicine in 2030: from IVF clinic to intelligent reproductive health systems

Seven shifts that will define the next decade of reproductive medicine — AI-assisted embryology, male fertility moving to centre stage, evidence-based personalisation, fertility preservation, honest outcome reporting, and why trust will remain the strongest medicine.

Read the full pieceClose

Fertility care is on the cusp of one of the biggest shifts since the invention of IVF. The next decade will not be about more IVF cycles, more labs or more technology. It will be defined by a larger question: can reproductive medicine become more predictive, personalised, ethical, accessible and emotionally intelligent? The scale of the challenge is undeniable. The WHO says that almost one in six adults globally will experience infertility at some point in their life, demonstrating that infertility is not a niche issue but a major public health concern. At the same time, the fertility services market is anticipated to register a CAGR of 9.7% and reach USD 63.36 billion in 2030, up from USD 39.88 billion in 2025. But this is less about expanding the market. The real story is a metamorphosis.

The first big change is the emergence of AI-assisted IVF labs. Embryo selection, sperm assessment, time-lapse imaging, trigger timing, stimulation prediction and outcome counselling are all becoming data-driven. There is potential for AI to reduce inter-observer variation and standardise embryology decisions but the evidence is still evolving. A 2024 Nature Medicine randomised trial showed that deep-learning embryo selection reduced evaluation time but did not demonstrate non-inferiority for clinical pregnancy compared with morphology-based selection. It’s an important reminder: AI should not replace clinical wisdom but support it.

The second shift is the shift of male fertility from the wings to centre stage in fertility care. In roughly half of all cases of infertility, a male factor is present, either alone or together with another factor. In the future, fertility clinics will not focus on women when it comes to investigation, counselling and treatment planning . A semen analysis alone will not do. Couples should take into account DNA fragmentation, oxidative stress, lifestyle, endocrine status, varicocele, genetics, infections, environmental exposure and sexual health in fertility planning.

The third shift is evidence-based personalisation. “In the future the IVF protocol will not be ‘one protocol fits all Treatment will be based on the patient’s age, AMH, AFC, BMI, previous response, POSEIDON, ovarian sensitivity, sperm quality, endometrial receptivity, comorbidities and affordability. AI may assist decision support for clinicians in predicting response, preventing under or overstimulation, decreasing OHSS and increasing transparency of counselling.

The fourth shift is the introduction of fertility preservation as preventive reproductive medicine. The planned freezing of oocytes is becoming more ethically accepted, but patients need to be clearly counselled about uncertainties, success rates and long term expectations. The message should not be one of fear, but of awareness; reproductive time is biological capital and education at the right time can ward off emotional and financial crises later.

The fifth shift is intentionality around nutraceuticals and reproductive health. CoQ10, methylfolate, vitamin D, antioxidants, lifestyle modification, weight optimisation, sleep, metabolic control and anti-inflammatory nutrition are being discussed. But the future must be evidence led, not supplement led. ESHRE has been very clear that IVF add-ons should be offered with a clear discussion of safety and efficacy and what the realistic benefit is.

The sixth shift is standardising success in reporting. IVF success rates need to go beyond marketing figures. Quality indicators for transparency should include: live birth rate, cumulative live birth rate, age-specific outcomes, embryo-stage outcomes, miscarriage rate, cancellation rate, multiple pregnancy rate and patient-reported experience. The next generation of fertility clinics will compete on more than pregnancy rates, they will compete on truthfulness, safety, affordability, ethics and continuity of care.

Intelligent IVF laboratories: AI-driven embryology and quality systems

But the next fertility-care revolution may not be in the consultation room but in the embryology lab proper. In the past the success of IVF depended on strict control of the lab environment including temperature, pH, oxygen concentration, air quality, incubator stability and culture systems. But the IVF laboratory of the future will transition from a controlled environment to an intelligent environment. Artificial intelligence will continuously monitor laboratory parameters, foresee equipment problems before they become a problem, recognise subtle trends that impact embryo development, and conduct quality audits in real time. Advanced systems can generate digital copies of IVF laboratories (“Digital Twins”) to simulate and optimise workflow before implementation. Looking ahead, embryology units will increasingly depend on automated witnessing systems, AI-assisted embryo culture monitoring, predictive quality analytics and continuous performance dashboards to monitor fertilisation, blastocyst formation, implantation and live birth outcomes. Fertility centers will shift from retrospective audits to ongoing quality intelligence. The embryologist will evolve into a data enabled reproductive scientist, synergising clinical judgement, laboratory expertise, quality management and AI assisted decision support to achieve the ultimate goal of reproductive medicine, a healthy baby delivered safely, ethically and consistently.

This transformation will be centred in Europe and Asia-Pacific including India. Demand will be propelled by increasing awareness of infertility, late parenthood, urban stress, PCOS, endometriosis, male factor infertility and increased access to ART. But the biggest opportunity for India is not only on the volume side. It combines clinical excellence with compassion, affordability, digital counselling, assisted decision making by AI and culturally sensitive care to create fertility systems. The future fertility clinic will be more than an IVF centre. "It's going to be a centre of reproductive smartness." It will diagnose early, counsel deeply, personalise scientifically, use AI responsibly, protect embryos ethically, include men equally and treat patients with dignity.

The next decade belongs to fertility specialists who understand one thing: Technology can make things more precise, but trust will be the strongest medicine in fertility care. The fertility centers that will succeed are the ones that can translate lab data into actionable intelligence, not the ones with the most infrastructure, while still keeping the human touch central to fertility care.

These seven seismic shifts – AI-assisted decision making, increased attention to male fertility, personalised reproductive medicine, fertility preservation, evidence-based wellness integration, transparent outcome reporting and intelligent embryology laboratories – collectively herald a new age of reproductive medicine. The future of fertility is not about doing more IVF cycles, it’s about smarter systems, better data, higher quality standards and more personalised. The next decade of reproductive healthcare will be defined by fertility centers that innovate but hold on to ethics, empathy and patient trust. Fertility Medicine 2030 will do more than get patients pregnant. This enables them to make better reproductive choices, sooner, safer, and with greater confidence than ever before.

Women’s Health 2030: beyond fertility treatment

The future of women’s health is inseparable from the future of fertility medicine. Reproductive medicine is just one part of a woman’s biological journey through life. By 2030, women's health will shift from fragmented care to integrated, preventive and life-stage-based care.

There will be a need for early reproductive health education.” Young women should be aware of menstrual health, PCOS, endometriosis, ovarian reserve, contraception, fertility windows, sexually transmitted infections, lifestyle risks and when to see a doctor.

Menstrual and hormonal health will offer a diagnostic window. Irregular cycles, severe dysmenorrhoea, acne, weight gain, abnormal bleeding, pelvic pain or painful intercourse should not be normalised for years. AI-powered symptom trackers, ultrasound devices, hormonal dashboards and structured clinical pathways may aid earlier detection of PCOS, endometriosis, fibroids, adenomyosis, thyroid disorders, hyperprolactinaemia and metabolic risk.

Women’s health will also be a mix of fertility and metabolic health. Reproductive outcomes are not independent of PCOS, obesity, insulin resistance, diabetes, hypertension, fatty liver, sleep disturbance, and inflammation. Future women’s health clinics will combine fertility counselling with metabolic medicine, nutrition, movement, sleep, mental health and long-term cardiovascular risk reduction.

Reproductive mental health will also be critical. Infertility, miscarriage, repeated IVF failure, pregnancy uncertainty, postpartum stress, perimenopause and sexual health concerns carry a deep emotional weight. Counselling, psychological support, grief care and relationship-sensitive communication should not be optional extras. They need to be central to reproductive health care.

The future women’s health centre will cover a woman from adolescence, fertility, pregnancy, postpartum, perimenopause, menopause and healthy ageing. That is the greater promise of Women’s Health 2030: not episodic care, but lifelong reproductive intelligence.

Fertility is not the science of making babies; it is the art of restoring possibilities and preserving the reproductive time.Dr Shyam N Gupta, MD

When hope refused to give up

A couple, eleven years of marriage, six clinics and two failed IVF cycles — and what their daughter taught me about the part of fertility medicine that never appears in a protocol.

Read the full pieceClose

In the journey of medicine, there are many patients we treat, many procedures we perform, and many reports we analyze. Yet, only a few stories stay with us forever—stories that remind us why we chose this profession in the first place. One such story from my career as a fertility specialist continues to shape the way I look at infertility, human resilience, and the deep emotional responsibility we carry as doctors.

This incident took place nearly a two years ago, around August 2023, when I was already several years into my practice in reproductive medicine. By then, I had treated hundreds of couples struggling with infertility, but that particular case taught me something beyond textbooks, protocols, and medical algorithms.

A couple walked into my clinic one quiet afternoon. The husband was calm but visibly tired, and the wife carried a silence that only someone who has endured repeated disappointments can understand. They had been married for 11 years and had visited more than six fertility clinics across different cities. They had undergone multiple treatments—ovulation induction, several cycles of IUI, and even two unsuccessful IVF attempts.

By the time they reached my clinic, they were emotionally exhausted and financially drained. What struck me the most was not their medical file—which was nearly two inches thick—but the hesitation in their voice when they spoke. It was the hesitation of people who had been told many times that their chances were very low.

After reviewing their reports carefully, I noticed that the wife had diminished ovarian reserve and the husband had moderately compromised sperm parameters. On paper, it was a difficult combination. The probability of success was not very encouraging, and any responsible doctor would have to explain that honestly.

But infertility is not only about numbers and probabilities. It is also about people.

When I asked them about their journey, the wife quietly shared something that deeply moved me. She said, “Doctor, every time we start treatment, we begin with hope. But when it fails, everyone around us behaves as if we have failed as people.”

Those words stayed with me.

In many parts of society, infertility is not just a medical condition—it becomes a social burden. Families ask questions. Relatives give advice. Sometimes blame is placed unfairly. The couple had faced all of this, yet they were still willing to try once more.

After detailed counselling, we decided to proceed with another IVF cycle, but this time with a more personalized approach. Instead of simply repeating a standard protocol, we carefully evaluated ovarian stimulation strategies, optimized the husband’s sperm quality with medical and lifestyle interventions, and focused on improving embryo selection.

I remember the stimulation phase very clearly. It was challenging because the wife responded with only a few follicles. Every scan felt like a delicate balance between medical judgment and emotional reassurance. On the day of egg retrieval, we managed to retrieve three oocytes. In many clinics, such a number might discourage both doctors and patients.

But in fertility treatment, even a single egg can hold a universe of possibility.

When the embryology report came the next day, two embryos had fertilized normally. Those embryos became the center of hope for that couple—and for us as a medical team.

A few days later, we performed the embryo transfer. It was a quiet moment in the IVF laboratory, but emotionally very powerful. I remember telling them gently, “Now the most important medicine is patience.”

The two-week wait after embryo transfer is perhaps the most emotionally intense phase for couples undergoing IVF. Every small symptom raises hope, every silence raises anxiety. During that time, the wife called the clinic several times—not with complaints, but simply to ask if everything was going fine.

Then came the day for the beta-hCG test. I was in the clinic when the report arrived. The result was positive.

In medical practice, we see many positive pregnancy tests. But some results carry a deeper emotional meaning. I still remember the moment we called the couple to share the news. There was silence on the phone for a few seconds, and then the wife began to cry.

Not out of sadness—but out of relief.

Pregnancy after infertility treatment is always handled with caution. The first ultrasound is often more emotional than the pregnancy test itself. When we performed the scan at around six weeks, we saw something every fertility doctor hopes to see—a small flickering heartbeat.

That moment was magical. For a fertility doctor, the most beautiful sound in the world is not applause—it is the first heartbeat of a long-awaited life.

The husband, who had remained composed throughout the journey, suddenly folded his hands in gratitude. The wife simply looked at the screen as if she wanted to memorize that tiny heartbeat forever.

The pregnancy progressed well, and months later, they welcomed a healthy baby girl.

But the story does not end there.

About a year after the delivery, the couple visited the clinic again—this time carrying their daughter in their arms. The baby was cheerful, curious, and completely unaware of the long journey that had brought her into the world.

The mother said something that touched me deeply. She said, “Doctor, for you it may have been one case among many, but for us it was our entire life.”

That sentence changed something within me.

As doctors, we often focus on clinical outcomes, success rates, protocols, and research data. These are all important. But sometimes we forget that behind every case file is a family whose entire emotional world revolves around that outcome.

That incident transformed the way I practice fertility medicine.

From that day onward, I began to focus even more on personalized fertility care—not just medically but emotionally. I started spending more time counselling couples, helping them understand their treatment journey, and ensuring they never felt alone in the process.

Infertility treatment is not merely about achieving pregnancy. It is about restoring hope, dignity, and emotional strength to couples who often feel defeated by circumstances beyond their control.

Over the past 10–12 years in this field, I have seen science advance tremendously—better IVF techniques, improved embryo culture systems, genetic testing, artificial intellegence and personalized stimulation protocols. But despite all these technological advances, the most powerful element in fertility treatment remains the human connection between doctor and patient.

That particular couple reminded me that medicine is not only about solving biological problems; it is also about standing beside people during some of the most vulnerable moments of their lives.

Every time I see that little girl during follow-up visits, I am reminded of something important: sometimes the greatest success in medicine is not the procedure we perform, but the hope we help someone rediscover.

And that is what continues to inspire me every day as a fertility specialist. A fertility specialist is not just a doctor of hormones and embryos; he is a guardian of dreams that couples have carried silently for years.

What couples ask before their first appointment

Straight answers about choosing a fertility specialist in Bengaluru, what happens after a failed cycle, and what to bring to a first consultation.

How do I choose a fertility specialist in J.P. Nagar, Bengaluru?
Look at three things rather than advertising. First, whether the doctor personally handles your kind of case — routine IVF and repeated failure are different problems. Second, whether they publish or teach, which shows the work is examined by peers. Third, whether the first consultation is spent explaining rather than selling. My IVF has already failed. What would be done differently?
A failed cycle is a question, not a verdict. Before transferring again the aim is to find the reason: embryo quality and genetics, endometrial receptivity and thickness, the immune picture, undiagnosed male factor such as high DNA fragmentation, and conditions like adenomyosis or chronic endometritis that are easy to miss. Only once there is an explanation does the next protocol get planned — repeating the same cycle and hoping for a different result is rarely the right answer.
What is recurrent implantation failure, and how is it investigated?
It usually means good-quality embryos have failed to implant across several transfers. Investigation covers the uterine cavity by hysteroscopy, chronic endometritis (CD138 testing), endometrial thickness and receptivity, thyroid and metabolic factors, immune and clotting evaluation, and a full re-look at the male side. Dr Gupta has published on GnRH agonist pre-treatment and endometrial CD138 plasma cell density in recurrent implantation failure and adenomyosis.
What is lymphocyte immunisation therapy (LIT), and who is it for?
LIT immunises the woman with her partner's lymphocytes, with the aim of shifting the immune response that may be rejecting the pregnancy. It is considered in selected couples with unexplained recurrent miscarriage after other causes are excluded, not offered routinely. Dr Gupta has published live birth outcomes after low-dose LIT with partner lymphocytes in Fertility & Reproduction, and presented the work at ESHRE. Evidence in recurrent implantation failure remains less settled than in recurrent miscarriage, and that distinction is explained before treatment.
There is no sperm in the semen sample. Is treatment still possible?
Often yes. Azoospermia may be obstructive or non-obstructive, and sperm can frequently be recovered surgically by PESA or TESA and used for ICSI. Around 500 surgical sperm retrievals have been performed at this centre. Dr Gupta holds a Diploma in Male Infertility from ANDROFERT, Brazil, and has published on PESA-retrieved sperm in high-risk male factor ICSI.
Do I need IVF, or would IUI be enough?
IUI can be reasonable with open tubes, adequate sperm and a younger partner, and it is far less invasive. IVF becomes the better route with tubal disease, significant male factor, low ovarian reserve, advanced age, endometriosis, or after IUI has failed several times. The honest answer depends on your tests, not on a general rule — which is why the first consultation is diagnostic rather than prescriptive.
What should I bring to a first consultation?
Everything you already have: previous cycle summaries and discharge notes, semen analyses, hormone profiles including AMH, thyroid and prolactin, HSG or ultrasound reports, hysteroscopy or laparoscopy notes, and details of medication used in earlier cycles. A consultation with the old records in the room is far more productive than one that starts from scratch.
Which conditions does Dr Gupta treat most often?
Recurrent implantation failure, recurrent miscarriage and reproductive immunology, male factor infertility including surgical sperm retrieval, PCOS, endometriosis, thin and refractory endometrium, and fertility-preserving hysteroscopic and laparoscopic surgery. Routine IVF, ICSI and IUI are part of the practice, but most referrals concern couples for whom something has already gone wrong more than once.
